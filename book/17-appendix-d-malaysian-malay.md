# Appendix D: Indonesian vs. Malaysian Malay — Affixation Differences

## Why this is an appendix, not a chapter

Indonesian (*Bahasa Indonesia*) and Malaysian Malay (*Bahasa
Malaysia*) are two standardized registers of the same language,
diverged mainly through separate 20th-century standardization (Dutch-
administered vs. British-administered) rather than through deep
grammatical split. The core affix **forms** this book teaches —
*meN-*/*peN-* nasal assimilation (Ch. 2, §2.2), *ber-*/*ter-*/*per-*
r-initial dissimilation (§2.3), and the whole inventory of circumfixes
in Chapters 4–10 — are shared between the two standards. There's no
separate forms table to teach here; a Malaysian speaker and an
Indonesian speaker build *menulis*, *ditulis*, *kebersihan*, and
*pertanian* the same way.

What actually differs is **usage**: which of two shared tools
(mainly *di-* vs. *ter-*) gets reached for, and how far one word's
(*kena*) range extends. That's thinner, patchier material than a
forms-table chapter, and the sourcing behind it is thinner too — see
the reliability note at the end before relying on specifics here.

## *ter-* carries more weight relative to *di-* in Malaysian Malay

Chapter 5 of this book teaches Indonesian *di-*/*ter-* as a fairly
clean split: *di-* for a deliberate, statable agent (§5.2–§5.3),
*ter-* for accidental/stative/potential readings with no deliberate
agent (§5.6–§5.7). Corpus work on Standard Malay (Malaysia) describes
a *ter-* that leans further into that territory than Indonesian's
does, and that behaves more flexibly besides — appearing in
constructions that read as active just as easily as passive, on the
same verb:

> Lelaki itu **tertangkap** oleh polis.
> "The man **was caught** by the police." (passive-leaning reading)
> Polis **tertangkap** lelaki itu.
> "The police **caught** the man." (same verb, active-leaning reading
> — documented as attested in Malaysian Malay; the Indonesian
> equivalent of this second sentence would use *di-* or plain *meN-*,
> not *ter-*, to get an active reading)

Indonesian doesn't use *ter-* this way — the Ch. 5 split (deliberate
*di-* vs. accidental/stative *ter-*) is more rigid, and §5.6's
potential reading ("tidak terangkat," "couldn't be lifted") is about
as far as Indonesian's *ter-* stretches toward anything other than a
passive/stative reading.

## *kena* carries an extra "have to" sense in Malaysian Malay

*kena* (lit. "to be hit, to incur") functions in both standards as an
adversative-passive-adjacent marker, roughly parallel to English
"got" in "got arrested" — a passive-like reading with a negative or
unwelcome flavor, distinct from both *di-* and *ter-*. This book
doesn't otherwise cover *kena* (it's a word, not an affix, so it falls
outside this book's scope the same way *tidak* or *sudah* do). It's
worth a mention here specifically because Malaysian Malay extends
*kena* to also mean "have to, must" (an obligation sense), a use
Indonesian doesn't make of the same word.

## What this means practically

If you've learned this book's Ch. 5 *di-*/*ter-* split and find
yourself reading or listening to Malaysian Malay, expect *ter-* to
show up more often, and in a wider range of sentence shapes, than
Indonesian usage would predict — don't assume you've misparsed a
sentence just because *ter-* is doing work you'd expect *di-* or
plain *meN-* to do in Indonesian.

## Reliability note

This appendix rests on noticeably thinner sourcing than the rest of
the book. A search for a single, authoritative, systematic comparison
of Indonesian and Malaysian Malay affixation turned up none — the
claims above are assembled from separate corpus studies of each
variety individually (one on Standard Malay *ter-* specifically, one
on Malay/Indonesian voice systems more broadly) plus one lower-
reliability general-audience source, not from one scholar's direct
side-by-side comparison. Treat the *direction* of the claims here
(Malaysian *ter-* is more flexible; *kena* has a broader range in
Malaysian) as reasonably well-supported, and any specific detail
beyond that (exact frequencies, register restrictions, dialectal
variation within Malaysia itself) as unverified. This appendix was
produced the same way as Appendix C's later additions: live web
searches run in conversation at the project owner's request, not
drawn from training-derived recall alone, and not checked against a
native Malaysian-Malay speaker.
