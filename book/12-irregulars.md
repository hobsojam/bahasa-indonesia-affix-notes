# Chapter 12: Irregulars & Lexical Idiosyncrasies

## 12.1 Overview

This chapter collects, in one place, every exception already flagged
as it came up in earlier chapters — roots that defy a rule you'd
otherwise expect to apply cleanly, meanings that drifted away from
what the affix "should" mean, and combinations that are simply blocked
for a specific root. None of this is new material; it's a catalog, so
you have one place to check instead of hunting back through several
chapters. Each entry links back to where it was first introduced.

## 12.2 The *kerja* cluster: one root, three irregularities

*kerja* ("work") is the single most irregular root in this book,
deviating from the default rule in three separate derivations:

| Form | Expected (regular) | Attested | Rule it breaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| *ber-* + *kerja* | *berkerja* | **bekerja** ("to work") | §2.3/§6.2: *r*-dissimilation doesn't apply (*kerja* isn't *r*-initial) |
| *pe-* + *kerja* | *pengerja* (*k*-drop, §2.2) | **pekerja** ("worker") | §8.4: expected nasal assimilation doesn't apply |
| *per-* + *kerja* + *-an* | *perkerjaan* | **pekerjaan** ("job, occupation") | §9.6: *r*-dissimilation doesn't apply, same as *ber-* |

Note that *kerja*'s **regular** *meN-...-kan* form is unaffected:
*mengerjakan* ("to do/work on something") follows the ordinary *k*-drop
nasal assimilation rule exactly as expected. The irregularity is
specific to these three derivations, not a general property of the
root.

## 12.3 *belajar*: an irregular *ber-* form from a vowel-initial root

*ajar* ("teach/learn") gives **belajar** ("to study, learn"), not the
regular *berajar* — even though *ajar* starts with a vowel, not *r*,
so the r-dissimilation rule (§2.3) can't explain it either. This is a
standalone lexical exception, like *bekerja* (§12.2), not an instance
of any rule given elsewhere in this book.

Its *pe-* nominal form inherits the same irregular stem rather than
reverting to the regular vowel-initial pattern: the person who studies
is **pelajar** ("student, pupil"), built on the irregular *(be)lajar*
stem — not *pengajar* (which instead exists, regularly, from bare
*ajar*, meaning "teacher, instructor" — see §8.3.1/§12.4).

## 12.4 Meaning splits where two affixes on the same root diverge
sharply

Sometimes a root takes two different circumfixes, each with a clean
derivation, but the resulting meanings are specific nouns/verbs that
don't reduce to "same idea, different grammatical shape" — you have to
know both forms as separate vocabulary:

| Root | Form A | Form B | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| *ajar* (teach/learn) | **pengajar** — "teacher" (regular *peN-*, agent of *mengajar*) | **pelajar** — "student" (irregular stem, §12.3) | Easy to confuse; they mean opposite roles. |
| *temu* (meet/find) | **penemuan** — "a discovery" (*pe-...-an*, §9.6) | **pertemuan** — "a (formal) meeting" (*per-...-an*, §9.6) | Same root, unrelated end meanings. |
| *kerja* (work) | **pengerjaan** — "execution of a task" (*pe-...-an*, regular) | **pekerjaan** — "job, occupation" (*per-...-an*, irregular, §12.2) | Easy to mix up; different register/specificity. |

## 12.5 Meaning drift: formal/figurative senses that outrun the literal
root

Covered first in §3.3.4: a *meN-* (or other affixed) form can pick up
a specialized, often figurative sense while the bare root stays purely
literal.

> *makan* (bare: "to eat," literal) → **memakan**: still "to eat," but
> also "to take, consume" in a figurative/formal sense — *memakan
> waktu* ("to take time"), *memakan korban* ("to claim victims")

A related but distinct kind of drift: a whole nominalized form
specializing into an institution-level meaning well beyond its literal
root sense, as already noted for *per-...-an* in §9.5 — *usaha*
("effort") → **perusahaan**, "a company/business enterprise," not
merely "the result of effort."

A third example, worth calling out on its own because the drift is
total rather than figurative-alongside-literal: *perintah* ("order,
command") takes *pe-* to give **pemerintah**, which in modern
Indonesian means "government" (the institution), not "one who gives an
order" in any literal, compositional sense a learner would predict
from the parts alone.

## 12.6 Blocked combinations: when the expected bare form just isn't a
word

Covered first in §3.3.3: some roots don't form a natural word with the
"default" affix alone, and need a specific extension before they mean
anything.

> *duduk* (to sit) → *menduduk* ✗ (not a real word) — needs *-i*:
> **menduduki** ✓ "to occupy" (e.g. a position, a territory)
> *naik* (to go up) → *menaik* (marginal, limited use) — needs *-kan*
> or *-i* for its common meanings: **menaikkan** ("to raise"),
> **menaiki** ("to ride, board")

There's no general rule predicting *which* roots are blocked like
this — it has to be checked per root, the same way the *pe-...-an* vs.
*per-...-an* split does (§9.6).

## 12.7 How to use this chapter

Treat this chapter as a reference you return to, not something to
memorize up front. The underlying pattern worth internalizing is
narrower than "Indonesian affixation has lots of exceptions": almost
every irregularity cataloged here either involves the single root
*kerja* (§12.2), the single root *ajar* (§12.3), or the lexically
arbitrary *pe-...-an*/*per-...-an* split (§9.6, §12.4) — it's a short
list of recurring trouble spots, not an unbounded one.
