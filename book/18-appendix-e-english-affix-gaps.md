# Appendix E: Where's the English Affix? A Contrastive FAQ

## Why this appendix exists

If English is the language you're translating this system against in
your head, some questions come up naturally: *where's the Indonesian
un-? Where's the plural -s? What happened to -ly?* This book hasn't
skipped these by accident. Indonesian's affix system is organized
around different categories than English's (voice and nominalization
are load-bearing here; number and tense barely register), so several
whole categories of English affix have no native Indonesian
counterpart at all, while a few do have one, just not where an
English speaker would instinctively look.

This appendix sorts the common English affixes into three buckets, so
you can look up "where did X go" directly instead of re-deriving it
from Chapters 1–12.

## Bucket 1: No Indonesian affix at all, native or borrowed

These English categories are handled by separate words or by context,
full stop. There's nothing bound to find, in any register.

| English affix | Example | How Indonesian does it instead |
|---|---|---|
| Plural *-s* | "books" | Reduplication (*buku-buku*), a numeral (*lima buku*, "five books"), or just context. No plural affix exists. |
| Past tense *-ed*, 3rd-person *-s*, future *will* | "walked," "walks," "will walk" | Separate aspect particles — *sudah* ("already"), *akan* ("will"), *sedang* ("currently"), *telah* ("already," formal) — not affixes. Already flagged as out of scope in §1.1. |
| Comparative *-er* | "bigger" | Periphrastic: *lebih* ("more") or *kurang* ("less") before the adjective, with *daripada*/*dari* ("than") introducing the thing compared — *lebih besar daripada itu*, "bigger than that." |
| Adverb *-ly* | "quickly" | No productive affix. Either the adjective form does double duty unchanged, or a periphrastic *secara*/*dengan* + noun construction is used. |

**The comparative/superlative asymmetry is worth pausing on.** This
book's Ch. 5 §5.8 already covers *ter-* as a real, native superlative
affix (*terbesar*, "biggest"). The comparative half of that same
paradigm (*-er*, "bigger") has no affix at all, native or otherwise —
it's *lebih besar*, full stop. English marks both ends of the
degree scale with an affix; Indonesian only marks one end.

## Bucket 2: A borrowed, bound affix exists, but it's formal/academic register, not native morphology

Indonesian has absorbed a cluster of bound affixes from Dutch (via
colonial-era academic and administrative vocabulary) and Sanskrit
(via older religious/literary borrowing), several of which land
suspiciously close to familiar English endings — because English
borrowed the same Latin/Greek or Sanskrit-adjacent material through
its own separate route. These are real, bound, and productive within
formal registers, but they're a layer bolted onto the language
separately from the native *me-*/*ber-*/*pe-*/*ke-* system Chapters
2–12 teach.

| English affix | Indonesian affix | Origin | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| *un-*/*non-*/*dis-* (negation) | *non-* | borrowed (Dutch/English, via Latin) | *nonaktif* ("inactive"), *nonformal* ("informal"), *nonfiksi* ("nonfiction") |
| *-ize*/*-ization* | *-isasi* | Dutch *-isatie* | *modernisasi* ("modernization"), *globalisasi* ("globalization"), *swastanisasi* ("privatization") |
| *-ism*/*-ist*/*-ity* | *-isme* / *-is* / *-itas* | Dutch *-isme* / *-isch* / *-iteit* | *nasionalisme* ("nationalism"), *nasionalis* ("nationalist"), *aktivitas* ("activity") |
| *pre-* | *pra-* | Sanskrit *pra-* (via Old Javanese) | *prasejarah* ("prehistory"), *prasekolah* ("preschool") |
| *post-* | *pasca-* | Sanskrit *paścā* | *pascasarjana* ("postgraduate"), *pascapanen* ("post-harvest"), *pascakolonial* ("postcolonial") |
| *anti-* | *anti-* | borrowed directly | *antivirus*, *antikorupsi* ("anticorruption") |

Two cautions with this whole bucket:

1. **Everyday default stays periphrastic.** *non-* doesn't replace
   *tidak* as the normal way to negate something — you'd never say
   *nonbahagia* for "unhappy," only *tidak bahagia*. These loan
   affixes fill a narrower, more technical/formal niche alongside the
   native periphrastic default, not instead of it.
2. **They don't play by Ch. 2's rules.** None of these nasal-assimilate
   or r-dissimilate the way *meN-*/*peN-*/*ber-*/*ter-*/*per-* do.
   *non-* stays *non-* regardless of the next sound; there's no
   *nombantu* parallel to *membantu*. Treat this bucket as a separate,
   simpler system bolted on top, not an extension of Chapters 2–12's
   rules.

(*pasca-* has a curious footnote: its ultimate Sanskrit root and
English *post-*'s ultimate Latin root both trace back to the same
Proto-Indo-European word for "behind, after." That's a coincidence of
deep shared ancestry, not evidence of borrowing in either direction —
Indonesian picked *pasca-* up from Sanskrit directly, millennia after
the Indo-European family split, not from English or Latin.)

## Bucket 3: Partial or asymmetric coverage — a native affix you already learned is quietly doing part of the job

A few English affixes turn out to be *already covered*, by a chapter
you've read for a different reason — you just didn't know that's what
it was doing.

**"Where's *-able*/*-ible*?"** You already have it: *ter-*'s potential
reading (§5.7) plus ordinary negation gets you most of the way there.
"Unbreakable" is *tidak terpecahkan* (lit. "not able-to-be-broken").
There's no single affix that means "-able" the way *ter-* alone means
"accidental passive," but the *combination* of *ter-* + negation is
the real, working equivalent — don't go looking for a separate
dedicated affix once you recognize this.

**"Where's *-ful*/*-less*?"** Partially covered, asymmetrically.
*-ful* overlaps with *ber-*'s "to have X" reading (§6.3.1):
*berbahaya* ("dangerous," lit. "has-danger") does roughly the job of
"harmful." *-less* has no systematic native counterpart the same
way — Indonesian reaches for *tidak* + the *ber-* form (*tidak
berbahaya*, "harmless") or *tanpa* ("without") + noun instead of a
single bound suffix.

**"Where's agentive *-er*?"** This is really two different English
suffixes that happen to be spelled the same way, and only one of them
has an Indonesian counterpart. Agent-noun *-er* ("writer," "teacher")
is *pe-* (Ch. 8) — fully covered. Comparative *-er* ("bigger") is
Bucket 1 above — not covered by any affix at all. If you're looking
for "the Indonesian *-er*," make sure you know which *-er* you mean
before concluding there's a gap.

## Why none of Bucket 2 or 3 is in Appendix A

Appendix A indexes the affixes this book actually teaches as part of
Indonesian's native system (Chapters 2–12). The loan affixes in
Bucket 2 are real and bound, but — like *kena* in Appendix D — they
sit outside that native system by this book's own scope decision, so
adding them to Appendix A would blur the line between "what this book
teaches you to produce" and "what exists in the language more
broadly." Bucket 3 isn't a new affix at all, just a different lens on
affixes already indexed there (*ter-*, *ber-*, *pe-*).

## Reliability note

The loanword etymologies and example words in Bucket 2 (*-isasi* from
Dutch *-isatie*, *-isme*/*-is*/*-itas* from Dutch *-isme*/*-isch*/
*-iteit*, *pra-* from Sanskrit *pra-* via Old Javanese, *pasca-* from
Sanskrit *paścā*) are sourced from Wiktionary's etymology entries and
a published study of loan suffixes in Indonesian, checked via live
search while drafting this appendix. The three-bucket *framework*
itself — and specifically the Bucket 3 claim that *ter-* + negation
functions as a working equivalent of *-able*, and that *ber-*
partially covers *-ful* — is this book's own synthesis, connecting
material already verified elsewhere in the book (Ch. 5 §5.7, Ch. 6
§6.3.1) rather than a claim drawn from any single comparative source.
Treat the bucket-1 and bucket-2 facts as reasonably solid and the
bucket-3 framing as a genuinely useful way to think about it, not a
distinction found in, or contradicted by, any reference grammar.

## Practice

For each English affix, say which bucket it falls into and give the
Indonesian equivalent (an affix, a periphrastic construction, or
"none, use context"):

1. The plural in "three cats."
2. *un-* in "unhelpful."
3. *-ization* in "modernization."
4. *-able* in "readable."

<details>
<summary>Answer key</summary>

1. Bucket 1 — no affix. *tiga kucing* ("three cats"); the numeral
   alone signals plurality, no affix on *kucing*.
2. Bucket 1 as the everyday default — *tidak membantu* ("not
   helpful/helping"). *non-* (Bucket 2) exists but wouldn't be used
   here; it's reserved for more formal/technical coinages.
3. Bucket 2 — *-isasi*: *modernisasi*.
4. Bucket 3 — *ter-* (§5.7) + the negation it combines with:
   *terbaca* ("readable," lit. "able-to-be-read"); negated,
   *tidak terbaca* would be "unreadable."
