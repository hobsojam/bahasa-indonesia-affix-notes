# Chapter 13: Synthesis — Parsing a Word

## 13.1 Overview

This chapter doesn't introduce new affixes. It's practice pulling
apart real, multi-layered words into root + affix(es), using every
pattern from Chapters 1–12 together, the way you'll actually meet them
in running text — stacked, not one at a time.

A general procedure for parsing an unfamiliar affixed word:

1. **Strip outer layers first.** Check for *ke-...-an* or *per-...-an*
   wrapping the whole word before anything else — these circumfixes
   are easy to miss if you start scanning from the middle.
2. **Identify the bare root.** Look it up if the result doesn't look
   like a word you recognize — you may be misparsing the boundary.
3. **Rebuild inward to outward.** Confirm each intermediate form is
   itself a real derived word, not just a string that happens to fit.
4. **Check Chapter 12** if a form looks phonologically "wrong" (e.g.
   missing an expected *r* or nasal) — it may be one of the catalogued
   irregular roots rather than an error in your parsing.

## 13.2 Worked example: a derivational family tree from one root

Starting from *hasil* ("result, yield"), four affixes stack across
voice and nominalization to build a small family of related words:

> *hasil* (root, "result, yield")
> → **menghasilkan** (*meN-...-kan*, Ch. 4: causative) — "to produce,
> yield"
> → **dihasilkan** (*di-*, Ch. 5: passive of the above) — "is
> produced"
> → **penghasilan** (*pe-...-an*, Ch. 9: pairs with the *meN-* verb) —
> "income, output"
> → **berhasil** (*ber-*, Ch. 6: intransitive, "to succeed") — a
> separate derivation straight from the root, not from *menghasilkan*
> → **keberhasilan** (*ke-...-an*, Ch. 9, wrapped around the *ber-*
> form rather than the bare root) — "success"

The last step is the one worth lingering on: *ke-...-an* here doesn't
attach to *hasil* directly (there's no *kehasilan*) — it attaches to
the already-derived *ber-* verb *berhasil*. Circumfixes stacking on
top of an intermediate derived word, rather than the bare root, is
normal; don't assume every circumfix you meet attaches straight to a
root just because the pattern tables in earlier chapters show roots as
the input.

## 13.3 Worked example: negation wrapped inside a circumfix

> **ketidakadilan** — "injustice"

Parse outward-in: *ke-...-an* (Ch. 9, abstract noun) wraps around
*tidak adil* ("not just" — *tidak*, "not," negating the adjective root
*adil*, "just," from Ch. 9's own *keadilan*, "justice"). The circumfix
isn't attaching to a single root at all here — it's attaching to a
whole negated adjective phrase, giving "the state of [not being
just]," i.e. "injustice," rather than some literal "un-justice-ness."

## 13.4 Worked example: a compound root inside a nominalizing circumfix

> **pemberitahuan** — "a notification, an announcement"

*beritahu* itself is a compound: *beri* ("to give") + *tahu* ("to
know") — "to inform" (lit. "to give knowing"). *pe-...-an* (Ch. 9)
wraps around that whole compound, nasal-assimilating on its first
sound (*b*-initial → *pem-*, per §2.2/§8.2) to give *pemberitahuan*.
Treat *beritahu* as a single unit once you've identified it — don't
try to re-derive the circumfix's nasal assimilation from *tahu*'s
initial sound; the compound's own first sound (*b*) is what governs
it.

## 13.5 Worked example: the *ke-...-an* experiencer trap, revisited

> Saya **kedatangan** tamu tak diundang semalam.
> "I **had an uninvited guest show up** on me last night."

This is the verbal/experiencer use of *ke-...-an* flagged as a trap in
§9.3, not the nominal "arrival" sense from the same word. The tell:
*kedatangan* here takes its own object (*tamu tak diundang*, "an
uninvited guest") the way a verb does, and the sentence's subject
(*saya*, "I") is the person something happened *to* — neither of which
a noun could do. Re-read §9.3 if this distinction isn't automatic yet;
it's one of the harder traps in the book precisely because the surface
form gives no visual cue that you're looking at the "wrong" reading.
