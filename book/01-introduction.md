# Chapter 1: Introduction

## 1.1 What this book covers

Bahasa Indonesia builds most of its vocabulary by layering **affixes**
onto a **root word** (*kata dasar*). The root carries the core meaning;
the affixes shift that meaning, change the word's grammatical category,
or change who or what the sentence is "about" (its **voice**). Learn the
affix system and a huge amount of the vocabulary you meet stops looking
like a wall of unrelated words and starts looking like a small set of
roots combined in predictable ways.

This book is narrowly scoped to that affix system: prefixes, suffixes,
circumfixes, and infixes, plus the handful of phonological rules
(mainly nasal assimilation and r-initial dissimilation) you need to
produce them correctly. It assumes you already have:

- basic Indonesian vocabulary and sentence structure,
- enough grammar to recognize a sentence's subject, verb, and object,
- no prior background in linguistics terminology (that's covered as it
  comes up, and collected in Appendix B).

It does **not** cover pronouns, question formation, tense/aspect
markers (*sudah*, *akan*, *sedang*...), numbers and classifiers beyond
what's needed for the *ke-* ordinal pattern, or general vocabulary
building. Those belong to a general grammar course, not this one.

---

**Sidebar: does any of this vary by ethnicity, religion, or social
hierarchy?**

Short answer: not really, and the "not really" is itself informative
about what kind of system this is. Standard Indonesian's affix rules
(the ones this book teaches) don't vary by who's speaking — a root
takes the nasal assimilation in §2.2 the same way whether the speaker
is Muslim or Christian, Javanese or Chinese-Indonesian, or addressing
a stranger versus a superior.

**Religion** shows up in *vocabulary*, not morphology: Muslim-context
Indonesian draws more on Arabic loanwords (*ibadah*, *insya Allah*,
*zakat*), Christian-context Indonesian more on Dutch/Portuguese-
derived ones (*gereja*, *misa*, *pendeta*). Once borrowed, those roots
take ordinary affixes exactly like any other root — *beribadah*
("to worship") is just *ber-* (§6.3.1) + *ibadah*, nothing special
about the affixation itself.

**Ethnicity** is subtler, but the real variable turns out to be
*language contact*, not ethnic identity as such. Javanese-Indonesian
bilingualism is the clearest documented case: Javanese has its own,
different affix system (more on exactly how different, next), and
there's real sociolinguistic research on how that shows up in
bilinguals' Indonesian — one strand even argues this book's own
*-el-*/*-em-*/*-er-* infixes (Chapter 11) were historically borrowed
from Javanese in the first place. That's a first-language-contact
effect, present in a Javanese-dominant speaker and absent in, say,
someone who identifies as ethnically Javanese but grew up
Indonesian-monolingual in Jakarta — it tracks which language you
learned affixation in first, not your ethnicity as a label. Jakarta
colloquial Indonesian (dropped *meN-*, the *nge-*/*-in* system covered
in Chapter 7 §7.5, discourse particles like *sih*/*dong*) is real too,
but it's a geographic, cross-ethnic urban register, not a marker of
any one ethnic group specifically — treat any claim that it is as
folk stereotype rather than linguistics.

**Social hierarchy** is where Javanese's "different affix system"
actually matters most, and where Indonesian's design as a national
lingua franca shows up clearest. Javanese has a grammaticalized
honorific system — three speech levels (*ngoko*, *madya*, *krama*)
chosen based on the relative status of speaker and addressee, where
*the affixes themselves differ by level*, not just the vocabulary;
the most elaborate level, *krama alus*, layers on its own separate
honorific words for the addressee's actions (*krama inggil*) and
self-deprecating words for the speaker's own (*krama andhap*).
Indonesian has nothing equivalent — this book's affixes don't change
based on who you're addressing, full stop, and that's not an
incomplete description; it's a real, often-cited structural
difference, part of why Indonesian succeeded as an egalitarian
lingua franca across hundreds of ethnic groups where adopting
Javanese wholesale would have forced every speaker to constantly
grammatically encode social rank. Indonesian politeness instead runs
through *word choice* — the pronoun *Anda* (formal "you") vs. *kamu*
(casual) vs. Jakarta-slang *lu*, or substituting a kinship/title term
for 2nd person entirely (*Pak*, *Bu*, *Mas*, *Mbak*) — never through
changing how a verb takes *meN-* or *di-*. (There's a documented
phenomenon, *kramanisasi* ["krama-ization"], where formal/political
Indonesian has absorbed something like a high/low tone split under
Javanese cultural influence — but that's register and word choice
drifting, not a second affix system growing underneath it.)

## 1.2 Root words (*kata dasar*)

A *kata dasar* is the smallest meaningful unit a word can be reduced to
once every affix is stripped off. Roots are usually one or two
syllables and belong to a word class on their own, even unaffixed:

| Root | Class (bare) | Gloss |
|---|---|---|
| *tulis* | verb-like | "write" |
| *besar* | adjective | "big" |
| *rumah* | noun | "house" |
| *makan* | verb-like | "eat" |

Indonesian doesn't mark verbs with person/tense endings the way English
or French do, so a bare root like *tulis* or *makan* can already stand
in as a verb in casual speech (*Saya makan nasi*, "I eat rice"). What
affixation adds on top of that bare-root baseline is precision: whether
the action is foregrounding the actor or the undergoer (voice), whether
it's deliberate or accidental, whether the result is a person, a tool,
or an abstract concept, and so on.

Everything in this book starts from a root and asks: *what does adding
this affix do to it?*

## 1.3 Word classes and what affixation changes

Three things an affix can change, independently of each other:

1. **Word class** — turning a verb root into a noun (*tulis* "write" →
   *tulisan* "writing/document"), or an adjective into a noun (*bersih*
   "clean" → *kebersihan* "cleanliness").
2. **Voice** — which participant in the event the sentence is built
   around: the actor (active, *me-*), the undergoer (passive, *di-*),
   or an undergoer with no implied agent (*ter-*). Covered in depth in
   Chapters 3–5.
3. **Argument structure / meaning shade** — whether the action is
   causative, directed at a location, repeated, reciprocal, and so on.
   This is mostly the job of the *-kan* and *-i* suffixes and the
   *ber-...-an* circumfix (Chapters 4, 6, 7).

Keep these three axes separate in your head. A single affix often does
more than one of these at once (e.g. *pe-...-an* changes both word class
*and* contributes a process/place reading), but the axes themselves
don't move together predictably, so treating "what changed" as one
lump tends to cause confusion later.

## 1.4 Affix types

| Type | Attaches | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Prefix | before the root | *me-* + *tulis* → *menulis* |
| Suffix | after the root | *tulis* + *-an* → *tulisan* |
| Circumfix | before **and** after the root, as one unit | *ke-* + *bersih* + *-an* → *kebersihan* |
| Infix | inside the root, after its first consonant | *g* + *-el-* + *embung* → *gelembung* |

Circumfixes deserve a specific warning: *ke-...-an* is not "the prefix
*ke-*, and separately, the suffix *-an*, that happen to both show up."
It's a single unit of meaning that wraps the root. You'll see this
matter later when, for example, *ke-* alone (ordinal numbers, Chapter
10) and *ke-...-an* (abstract nouns, Chapter 9) turn out to have
nothing to do with each other beyond sharing three letters.

Infixes are a tiny, frozen set in modern Indonesian — covered on their
own in Chapter 11 — and are not something you can use to coin new
words.

## 1.5 Notation conventions used in this book

- Indonesian words and morphemes: *italics*.
- English glosses: "in quotes."
- A hyphen marks an affix boundary on a bound morpheme: *me-*, *-an*,
  *ke-...-an*. A root word written alone never carries a hyphen.
- → marks derivation: *tulis* → *menulis*.
- ✓ marks a grammatical, attested form; ✗ marks one that isn't a real
  word (or isn't natural) even though it looks like it should parse.
- ↔ marks a voice-pair relationship (active ↔ passive) between two
  forms of the same root.
- Example sentences are glossed on the line below in "quotes," with the
  relevant affixed word **bolded** in both languages where that's
  unambiguous.

## 1.6 How each chapter is organized

Every chapter that covers one or more affixes follows the same shape:
an overview, the forms table (including any phonological rule),
numbered usage notes (one per distinct meaning or function), explicit
"trap" callouts wherever an Indonesian form could mislead you by looking
like something it isn't, a decision table when two affixes compete for
the same job, and a short practice set with a collapsible answer key.

Chapter 2 covers the two phonological rules (nasal assimilation,
r-initial dissimilation) you'll otherwise run into piecemeal across
several later chapters — read it before Chapter 3.
