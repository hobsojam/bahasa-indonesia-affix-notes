# Chapter 9: Nominalization II — Abstract & Result Nouns

## 9.1 Overview

Four patterns turn a root into an abstract or result noun. They don't
compete for the same job in the way *-kan*/*-i* do (Chapter 4) — each
has its own typical input and output — but two of them, *pe-...-an* and
*per-...-an*, do compete directly for the same semantic space on
overlapping roots, which is where most of the difficulty in this
chapter lives.

| Pattern | Typical root | Typical output | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *-an* | verb or noun | concrete result/product noun | *tulis* → **tulisan** ("writing, document") |
| *ke-...-an* | adjective (sometimes noun) | abstract quality/state noun | *bersih* → **kebersihan** ("cleanliness") |
| *pe-...-an* | verb (meN- pattern) | abstract process/place noun | *didik* → **pendidikan** ("education") |
| *per-...-an* | verb (ber- pattern) | abstract/collective noun | *tani* → **pertanian** ("agriculture") |

## 9.2 *-an*: result, product, or collective noun

*-an* attaches directly to the root (no prefix, no nasal assimilation)
to name the concrete result or product of an action, or a collective
grouping:

> *tanam* (plant) → **tanaman**: "a plant" (the result of planting)
> *temu* (meet/find) → **temuan**: "a discovery, a finding"
> *kirim* (send) → **kiriman**: "a package, a parcel" (the thing sent)
> *latih* (train) → **latihan**: "exercise, practice, a training
> session"

## 9.3 *ke-...-an*: abstract quality or state noun

*ke-...-an* most often attaches to an **adjective** root, naming the
abstract quality that adjective describes:

> *adil* (just, fair) → **keadilan**: "justice, fairness"
> *bersih* (clean) → **kebersihan**: "cleanliness, hygiene"
> *dekat* (near) → **kedekatan**: "closeness, proximity"
> *jauh* (far) → **kejauhan**: "distance" (also "too far," depending on
> context)

It also attaches to some noun or verb roots for an institutional or
event-like abstract sense:

> *datang* (to come) → **kedatangan**: "arrival, coming"

### Trap: *ke-...-an* isn't only a noun-forming circumfix

On certain verb roots, *ke-...-an* instead forms a **verb** describing
an unintentional or adverse experience — something that happens *to*
the subject, not a noun at all:

> Saya **kedatangan** tamu mendadak.
> lit. "I was arrived-upon by sudden guests." / "**I had guests show
> up on me** unexpectedly." — here *saya* ("I") is the subject, the
> one affected, and *kedatangan* functions as a verb ("to have
> [someone] arrive on you, unexpectedly") with *tamu mendadak* as its
> complement, not the noun "arrival" from the example above, where
> *kedatangan* itself was the subject.

Both readings exist on the same word, *kedatangan* — context (is there
a following noun phrase acting as the thing that "happened to" a
subject, or is *kedatangan* itself the subject/object of the
sentence?) is what tells them apart. This adverse/experiencer use is a
separate grammatical construction from the nominal pattern this
chapter is otherwise about; it's flagged here rather than developed in
full, since it's really part of the verb system, not nominalization.

## 9.4 *pe-...-an*: abstract process or place noun

*pe-...-an* nasal-assimilates exactly like bare *pe-* (§8.2), and
typically pairs with a root that already has a *meN-* verb form
(Chapter 3):

> *didik* (educate) → **pendidikan**: "education" (*d*-initial → *pen-*)
> *latih* (train) → **pelatihan**: "training, a training program"
> (*l*-initial → *pe-*, no insertion)
> *kirim* (send) → **pengiriman**: "sending, shipment, delivery"
> (*k*-initial → *peng-*, *k* dropped)

## 9.5 *per-...-an*: abstract or collective noun

*per-...-an* does **not** nasal-assimilate — it's a phonologically
distinct pattern from *pe-...-an*, not an allomorph of it — and
typically pairs with a root that has a *ber-* verb form (Chapter 6)
rather than a *meN-* one:

> *tani* (farmer root; cf. *bertani*, "to farm") → **pertanian**:
> "agriculture"
> *usaha* (effort; cf. *berusaha*, "to try, strive") → **perusahaan**:
> "company, enterprise" — note the semantic drift from "the result of
> making an effort" to "a business entity"; don't assume the *per-...-an*
> form always stays as literal as the root.
> *guru* (teacher) → **perguruan**: "institution of higher learning,"
> as in *perguruan tinggi* ("university")

Like the other *ber-*-family prefixes (§2.3), *per-* drops its *r*
before an *r*-initial root: *per-* + *resmi* ("official") →
**peresmian**, not *perresmian* — "an inauguration."

## 9.6 Choosing between *pe-...-an* and *per-...-an*

**Which roots take which is lexically determined, not predictable from
a general rule — this is one of the more genuinely idiosyncratic
corners of the affix system, and worth treating as a dictionary lookup
rather than a derivation.** The *meN-*-correlates-with-*pe-...-an* /
*ber-*-correlates-with-*per-...-an* pattern above is a real tendency and
a useful first guess, but it is a heuristic, not a guarantee — check an
actual dictionary entry before committing to a form you haven't
independently seen.

The clearest way to see both the heuristic *and* its limits is a root
that takes **both** circumfixes, with genuinely different meanings:

| Root | *pe-...-an* | Gloss | *per-...-an* | Gloss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *temu* (meet, find) | **penemuan** | "a discovery, invention" | **pertemuan** | "a (formal) meeting" |
| *kerja* (work) | **pengerjaan** | "the execution/completion of a task" | **pekerjaan** | "a job, occupation" |

*pekerjaan* is a further wrinkle on top of the lexical split: by the
regular rule in §9.5, *per-* + *kerja* + *-an* should surface as
*perkerjaan*, with no *r*-dissimilation expected since *kerja* doesn't
start with *r*. The attested word drops the *r* anyway — the same
irregularity already flagged for *bekerja* (§6.2) and *pekerja* (§8.4),
now showing up a third time in this root's nominal form. Treat *kerja*
as one root that's simply irregular across the board, rather than
updating the general rule to match it.

## 9.7 A note on sourcing this section

*pe-...-an* and *per-...-an* are exactly the kind of lexically
arbitrary split this book's source notes warn about: the reference
project this book cross-checks against (`pembilas/data/annotations.json`)
only tags *temu* with a single, somewhat conflated *pe_an* gloss
("discovery; invention; meeting (formal)") and has no separate tag for
*pertemuan* at all. *penemuan* and *pertemuan* are two distinct,
independently well-attested words with different meanings, not one
word with two glosses — the split in §9.6's table is sourced from
standard dictionary entries (Wiktionary/KBBI), not from that
annotation file. This is a useful concrete reminder of the project's
own guidance: treat an annotation as a corroborating data point, not
the final word, especially for exactly this kind of lexically-split
pair.
