# Chapter 7: Bare *-kan* and *-i*

## 7.1 Overview

*-kan* and *-i* don't require *me-* to attach. Dropped from a *meN-*
circumfix (Chapter 4), they can attach straight to the bare root,
keeping the same causative/benefactive (*-kan*) or locative/repetitive
(*-i*) meaning from Chapter 4 but losing the actor-focus marking that
*me-* adds. The result is most natural as an **imperative** (a command
aimed at "you," where marking the actor is redundant — the actor is
whoever you're talking to); outside of imperatives, using the bare form
in a statement is a colloquial register choice.

| Full *meN-* circumfix | Bare suffix | Register where bare is natural |
|---|---|---|
| *menuliskan* | *tuliskan* | imperative (any register); casual statement |
| *mengambilkan* | *ambilkan* | imperative (any register); casual statement |

## 7.2 Imperatives: bare is the default, not just casual

In a command, dropping *me-* and using the bare *-kan*/*-i* form is the
**normal, expected** pattern — not a colloquial shortcut. A command
with *me-* left on sounds stiff or odd:

> **Tuliskan** nama kamu di sini!
> "**Write down** your name here!"
> (not the stilted *Menuliskanlah nama kamu di sini!*)

> **Ambilkan** saya air, ya?
> "**Get** me some water, would you?"

> **Tutupi** jendela itu!
> "**Cover** that window!" (locative *-i*: the window is the surface
> acted on)

This holds regardless of how formal the overall context is — a formal
written instruction manual still uses bare imperative forms like this.

## 7.3 Declarative statements: bare is a colloquial register marker

Outside an imperative, dropping *me-* on a *-kan*/*-i* verb in an
ordinary statement is specifically a **casual/spoken-register**
feature, parallel to the bare-*meN-* colloquialism already noted in
§3.3.2:

> (formal) Saya **menuliskan** nama saya di sana.
> (casual) Saya **tuliskan** nama saya di sana.
> "I wrote my name down there."

The *-kan*/*-i* suffix itself doesn't change meaning here — only the
actor-focus prefix *me-* is being dropped, exactly as in §3.3.2. Formal
writing (news articles, official documents, textbooks) keeps the full
*meN-...-kan*/*meN-...-i* form in declarative statements; casual speech
freely drops it.

## 7.4 Trap: don't confuse a dropped-*me-* bare form with a root that
simply has no *meN-* form

Some roots' most natural verb form genuinely has no productive
*meN-...-kan*/*meN-...-i* counterpart in everyday use, so a bare *-kan*/
*-i* form you encounter isn't always "*me-* dropped" — sometimes it's
simply the only form that exists. Telling these apart isn't always
obvious from the surface form alone; when in doubt, check whether the
*meN-* version is independently attested as a real, used word before
assuming you're looking at a colloquial reduction rather than the base
form itself.

## 7.5 Jakarta colloquial Indonesian: *nge-* and *-in*

Everything so far in this chapter is standard Indonesian's own
*-kan*/*-i* suffixes, used without *me-*. **Jakarta colloquial
Indonesian** (documented as its own variety — see Sneddon's
*Colloquial Jakartan Indonesian*, and Hidajat's and Arka & Yannuar's
work on it specifically) goes a step further: it has its *own*
distinct active prefix, *nge-*, and its own suffix, *-in*, which don't
simply drop *me-* the way §7.2–§7.3 do — they're a parallel colloquial
system, generationally associated with younger adult speakers in
Jakarta specifically, not casual speech everywhere Indonesian is
spoken.

**Forms:** *nge-* attaches to a root with no nasal assimilation at
all, regardless of the root's first sound — a genuine simplification
of the *meN-* rule from §2.2/§3.2, not a variant of it:

| Root | Standard *meN-* (Ch. 3) | Colloquial *nge-* |
|---|---|---|
| *erti* (understand) | mengerti | **ngerti** |
| *omong* (talk) | — | **ngomong** |
| *kasih* (give) | memberi (different root) | **ngasih** |

**-in replaces both *-kan* and *-i***, collapsing the
causative/benefactive-vs-locative/repetitive distinction Chapter 4
spent §4.3–§4.5 teaching you to draw:

| Root | Standard equivalent (Ch. 4) | Colloquial *nge-...-in* |
|---|---|---|
| *kerja* (work) | mengerjakan ("to do/work on," *-kan*) | **ngerjain** |
| *kasih* (give) | memberikan ("to give to someone," *-kan*) | **ngasihin** |
| *tawa* (laugh) | menertawakan ("to laugh at," locative-leaning) | **ngetawain** |

### Trap: *-in* isn't "colloquial *-i*" — it does *-kan*'s job too

Because *-in* looks like a clipped *-i*, it's tempting to assume it
only covers *-i*'s locative/repetitive territory from §4.3.3–§4.3.4.
It doesn't — *ngerjain* and *ngasihin* above are covering *-kan*'s
causative/benefactive territory, not *-i*'s. The whole point of the
colloquial system is that it **doesn't** preserve the distinction
Chapter 4 is built around; don't try to reverse-engineer which
standard suffix a given *-in* form "really" corresponds to as if
there were a strict rule — check the gloss directly.

### *-in* bare, without *nge-*, in imperatives

Parallel to §7.2's bare *-kan*/*-i* in standard Indonesian, colloquial
*-in* also appears to drop *nge-* in direct commands:

> **Beresin** kamar lo!
> "**Clean up** your room!" (from *beres*, "tidy," + *-in*; no *nge-*)

This parallel is plausible — the same "actor focus is redundant in a
command aimed at 'you'" logic from §7.2 would predict it — but it's
the single least-verified claim in this section: the sources checked
for *nge-*/*-in* generally (§7.5 above) didn't specifically confirm
this imperative pattern, only the declarative use. Treat it as likely
rather than confirmed.

### Scope note

*nge-*/*-in* is its own, separately-documented system, not a form of
the register-dropping covered in §7.2–§7.4 — don't treat *ngerjain* as
"*mengerjakan* with the prefix dropped and the suffix clipped." This
book doesn't otherwise teach Jakarta colloquial Indonesian in any
depth; this section exists to flag that the system exists and name its
two main pieces, not to cover it comprehensively. See Appendix C for
how this section was sourced.

## Practice

For each sentence, identify whether the bare *-kan*/*-i* form is (a) an
imperative, where the bare form is the expected default, or (b) a
colloquial statement, where it's a register choice standing in for a
full *meN-* form:

1. **Siramkan** air ke tanaman itu!
2. Kemarin saya **siramkan** air ke tanaman itu.
3. **Dekati** dia perlahan-lahan!
