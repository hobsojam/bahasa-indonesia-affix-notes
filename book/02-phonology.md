# Chapter 2: Phonology You Need First

## 2.1 Overview

Two sound-change rules do almost all of the phonological work in this
book. Learn them once, here, and the rest of the book can just point
back at this chapter instead of re-explaining them every time they come
up:

1. **Nasal assimilation** — governs *meN-* (Chapter 3) and *peN-*
   (Chapter 8), where "N" stands for a nasal consonant that changes to
   match the root's first sound.
2. **r-initial dissimilation** — governs *ber-*, *ter-*, and *per-*
   (Chapters 5, 6, 9), which all drop their own *r* before an
   *r*-initial root to avoid two *r* sounds in a row.

A short note on syllable shape closes the chapter, since it's needed to
understand why infixes (Chapter 11) land where they do.

## 2.2 Nasal assimilation (*meN-*/*peN-*)

*me-* and *pe-* are written as if the nasal consonant were fixed, but
it isn't: it's a placeholder (conventionally written **N**) that
surfaces differently depending on the point of articulation of the
root's first sound. For some root-initial consonants, the root's own
sound is also replaced rather than kept alongside the nasal — this is
traditionally taught with the mnemonic that **K, P, S, T** are the
consonants that "disappear."

| Root starts with | *meN-* surfaces as | Root's initial kept? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *b, f, v* | *mem-* | yes | *bantu* → **membantu** ("to help") |
| *p* | *mem-* | **no** (p dropped) | *pukul* → **memukul** ("to hit") |
| *c, j, sy, z* | *men-* | yes | *cari* → **mencari** ("to search for") |
| *d* | *men-* | yes | *dengar* → **mendengar** ("to hear") |
| *t* | *men-* | **no** (t dropped) | *tulis* → **menulis** ("to write") |
| *g, h, kh* | *meng-* | yes | *gambar* → **menggambar** ("to draw") |
| *k* | *meng-* | **no** (k dropped) | *kirim* → **mengirim** ("to send") |
| vowel | *meng-* | yes | *ambil* → **mengambil** ("to take") |
| *s* | *meny-* | **no** (s dropped) | *sapu* → **menyapu** ("to sweep") |
| *l, m, n, r, w, y, ng, ny* | *me-* (no nasal inserted) | yes | *lihat* → **melihat** ("to see") |

Why the drops happen: *p, t, k, s* are all voiceless consonants
produced at roughly the same place in the mouth as the nasal that
replaces them (*p*→*m*, *t*→*n*, *k*→*ng*, *s*→*ny*), so the language
merges them into the nasal rather than pronouncing both. Consonants
that aren't close enough to a nasal's place of articulation (*b, d, g,
c, j...*) just get the nasal added in front, unreduced.

*pe-* (Chapter 8) undergoes exactly the same set of changes, substituting
*pe-* for *me-* in every row of the table above: *tulis* → *penulis*,
*kirim* → *pengirim*, *sapu* → *penyapu*.

**This table is the single most load-bearing fact in the whole book.**
Nearly every *me-*, *peN-*, *me-...-kan*, *me-...-i*, *pe-...-an* form
you'll meet follows it exactly. The irregular exceptions that don't are
few, lexically specific, and are cataloged together in Chapter 12
rather than scattered — if a form you derive from this table looks or
sounds wrong, it's worth checking there before assuming you made an
error.

## 2.3 r-initial dissimilation (*ber-*, *ter-*, *per-*)

*ber-*, *ter-*, and *per-* all share a single, simple rule: drop the
prefix's own *r* when the root itself begins with *r*, to avoid two
*r* sounds colliding at the boundary.

| Prefix | Before *r*-initial root | Result | Gloss |
|---|---|---|---|
| *ber-* | *rasa* ("feel") | **berasa** (not *berrasa*) | "to feel like, taste of" |
| *ter-* | *rasa* ("feel") | **terasa** (not *terrasa*) | "is felt, feels [like]" |
| *per-* | *resmi* ("official") | **peresmian** (not *perresmian*, via *per-...-an*) | "inauguration" |

Outside of an *r*-initial root, none of these three prefixes change
shape on account of the root's first sound — only *meN-*/*peN-* do
that. Don't over-generalize the *ber-*/*ter-*/*per-* rule to other
consonants; e.g. *ber-* + *cermin* ("mirror") is **bercermin**, not
*becermin* — *c* isn't *r*, so nothing reduces.

A small number of roots *do* lose the *r* from *ber-* or *per-* even
though they don't start with *r* (*kerja* → *bekerja*, not *berkerja*;
*ajar* → *belajar*, not *berajar*). These are real exceptions, not a
second phonological rule — they're memorized, lexically-specific forms,
covered in Chapter 12 rather than presented here as if they were
predictable.

## 2.4 Syllable basics

Indonesian root syllables are built around a vowel nucleus, with
0–1 consonants before it and 0–1 after it: (C)V(C). A root word is
typically one of these shapes stacked two or three times:

> *ru-mah* (CV-CVC), "house"
> *ma-kan* (CV-CVC), "eat"
> *ting-gal* (CVCC?... → CVC-CVC: *ting-gal*), "to stay/live"

The reason this matters for affixation specifically: an **infix**
(Chapter 11) is defined by where it lands relative to syllable
structure — right after the root's first consonant, before the vowel
that starts the first syllable. That's a fixed slot, not a free
insertion point, which is part of why the infix inventory in modern
Indonesian is small and closed rather than a productive pattern you can
apply to any root.
